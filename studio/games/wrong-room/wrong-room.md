# Wrong Room: the experience script

Four to eight people, about ten minutes a round. The host writes a fresh place and eight roles at it
(a content request; if it fails, the game uses one of its own ten places). Code deals every player a
role at the place, and deals one player nothing: the Stranger. The room must find the Stranger before
the Stranger works out where they are.

What makes it a different game from the others: a hidden role who is the *absence* of knowledge, a
strict enforced order of play, and a last-chance guess judged by the host. Nobody asks yes-or-no
questions of the host; players question each other.

## One round, as it plays

- Phones: everyone gets a peek card (press and hold to read it). Insiders read "You're at: Lighthouse.
  You're the keeper." The Stranger reads "You don't know where you are. Listen to the answers, and
  bluff." The TV says "Look at your phone. Keep it to yourself."
- Host: "Everyone here knows where they are, except one of you. Take turns asking each other
  questions to find who. Candle goes first." Two short sentences, then it stops.
- The order is code. The asker picks someone on their phone (never themselves, never the person who just
  asked them), then holds to talk and asks out loud. The person picked holds to talk and answers. Then
  they are the asker. Only the person who is up has a live mic; everyone else is muted, so a lobbying
  friend is not even heard. The TV shows who picks, who asks and who answers, the question count and a
  clock. A typed question or answer is allowed and appears on the TV, since nobody else can hear it.
- Each step has a thirty-second clock. A pick that doesn't come goes to the next person in seat order, a
  question never asked is dropped, an answer never given is skipped; the host says nothing about it,
  and the TV moves on.
- Anyone can press "Call the vote". When more than half the table has pressed, the vote opens; so does
  the vote when the twelve questions are spent or the seven minutes are up. The Stranger also has "I
  know where we are", which skips to their one guess.
- Host: "Time to vote. Who is the stranger?" Everyone votes on their phone. The most votes, with no tie,
  is the accused. A tie, or the wrong person, and the Stranger wins and the host reveals the place.
- A caught Stranger gets one guess, spoken or typed. Spoken, the host rules right or wrong against the
  real place; typed, the code checks it. Right and the Stranger wins anyway; wrong and the room wins.
- The reveal: the place on the TV, who the Stranger was, and a dry line. The host makes the Stranger look
  good whether they were caught or not.

## What the host is told, and never told

Told: the place, how many questions are used, and when to speak. Never told: who the Stranger is, or any
player's role. It speaks only at the start, when few questions are left, at the vote, and at the end;
each question and answer is a quiet fact that never wakes it. It rules only when the Stranger has spoken a
guess, and a ruling at any other moment is dropped. Roles are dealt by seat, independently of who the
Stranger is, so no other phone can tell who it is; a test checks every seat.

## Awkward cases

- Four players: the fewest. Three is a dead zone, since there are only two people to question, so the box
  says four.
- Eight players: the most, since a place has eight roles.
- Someone leaves: the Stranger leaving ends the round with the place revealed; anyone else leaving takes
  them out of the order (and restarts the vote if it was open). Under three left ends the round.
- The Stranger says nothing when asked to guess: the clock ends it, the room wins.
- The host mishears or lobbying: a ruling only counts after the Stranger actually spoke, and the room
  never reads the host's wording, only its ruling.
- The model fails to write a place: one of the game's ten fallbacks is used and nobody is told.

## What the app would need that does not exist yet

- A way for the host to speak up when a step stalls; today the TV simply moves on, quietly.
- A phone control that shows the turn order as a ring, not only "Waiting for Saucer".
